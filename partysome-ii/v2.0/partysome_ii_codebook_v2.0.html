PartySOME II Codebook

Version 2.0

PartySOME II measures how much attention political parties give to policy issues on social media. Each post published by a party's official account is classified into one of the 21 policy topics of the Comparative Agendas Project (CAP), or as No Policy Content, and the dataset counts these posts per party and month. Version 2.0 covers 268 parties in 22 countries on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, TikTok, Telegram and Bluesky, from each account's first post to October 2026, for a total of 6.80 million posts.

Version 2.0 replaces version 1.0 (219 parties in 18 countries, on Facebook, Twitter and Instagram, to December 2023). Version 1.0 is the version used in the companion paper published in West European Politics (Jan and Sattelmayer 2026), and all results reported there are based on it; it remains available unchanged for replication. The two versions differ in coverage and in classification; the last section of this codebook describes the differences.

The coverage file has one row per party, with party_id, country_name_short, party_name_short and one variable per platform, {platform}_cap_coverage. It takes the value “classified” when the party has posts on this platform in the dataset, and “no_account” when it has none, either because the party has no account on the platform or because no posts from its account are in the data.

The variable cap_label takes 22 values: Agriculture, Civil Rights, Culture, Defense, Domestic Commerce, Education, Energy, Environment, Foreign Trade, Government Operations, Health, Housing, Immigration, International Affairs, Labor, Law and Crime, Macroeconomics, Public Lands, Social Welfare, Technology, Transportation, and No Policy Content.

Classification

Each post is classified in two steps. First, a classifier fine-tuned for this project, mljn/policy-vs-other-v2, decides whether the post is about a policy issue. Posts it rejects are labelled No Policy Content. Second, posts it accepts are assigned one of the 21 CAP topics by poltextlab/xlm-roberta-large-pooled-cap-v3, used as published and not fine-tuned on this corpus.

Posts without text (empty, or the placeholder “NA” some sources use for an empty field) are labelled No Policy Content without being classified. Retweets and reposts are not party posts and are not included. For posts of Spanish parties, full stops and commas are removed from the text before the CAP classifier is applied; the policy/no-policy decision is made on the original text. This step is new in version 2.0 and is explained under Differences from version 1.0.

The text classified depends on the platform. On Facebook it is the post message; image text and the title or description of a shared link are not used. On Twitter it is the tweet, on Instagram the caption, on Telegram the message and on Bluesky the post text. On TikTok it is the caption, followed by the transcript of the video's speech when TikTok provides one (42.5% of TikTok posts; see Limitations).

The performance of both classifiers was evaluated on a random sample of about 5,400 posts, stratified by country and platform and coded by hand for policy content and CAP topic; the results are reported in Supplementary Material B of the companion paper. The sample is released as partysome_ii_validation_v2.0.csv: for each post, its identifier, country, party, platform, date and language, the hand-coded labels, and the labels predicted by the version 2.0 classifiers. Post texts are not included, in line with the platforms' terms of use. The hand-coded posts used to train the policy classifier and to validate both classifiers, with their texts, are available from the authors on request.

Because posts rejected by the first classifier receive no topic, the dataset provides only the recoded measure. The raw n_label and cap_share of version 1.0 are not included.

Panel structure

Each party's series runs from its own first month with a post to its own last month, and in the platform files from each party-platform's first to last month, not over a common window. Every month within that range has all 22 label rows, with zeros where there were no posts; a month with total_posts equal to 0 means the party posted nothing that month on that platform. There are no missing values.

Limitations

Users should be aware of the following.

  • Whether a party's replies to other accounts are included depends on how the tweets were collected. In the archive collections, which provide most tweets up to 2023, 5–20% of a year's tweets are replies to other accounts. Among tweets collected from parties' profile pages, which provide about half of the tweets in 2025 and most of those in 2026, fewer than 0.1% are. Part of the change in Twitter post counts after 2023 therefore reflects collection rather than party behaviour.
  • TikTok transcripts are available for almost no posts before 2022, about 10% in 2022, 30–37% in 2023 and 40–60% from 2024. Since posts with a transcript are classified on more text, the share of TikTok posts classified as policy content can rise over time for this reason alone.
  • Shares computed from months with few posts are noisy; weighting by total_posts or pooling months is advisable.

Differences from version 1.0

Users replicating or extending the analyses of the West European Politics paper should note the following differences. Version 2.0 is rebuilt from the project's database rather than derived from version 1.0, so the two can differ in which posts they contain (coverage) and in how the same post is labelled (classification). To measure both, every post of version 1.0 was matched to version 2.0, by post identifier and otherwise by party, platform, month and identical text. Coverage

  • Gaps documented in PartySOME. Many of the coverage gaps listed in the PartySOME codebook are filled. On Facebook, the periods missing from the CrowdTangle collection are filled for Italy (2011–2018), Hungary (2016–2018 and 2021), Norway (2015), the SNP (2010) and Podemos (from late 2022); the SPD's history, restricted at the time of the original collection, is recovered (102 posts in version 1.0, 4,585 in version 2.0); and UPyD, CiU, the PR (France), the SDLP, Alliance (Northern Ireland) and Scelta Civica, listed as not collected, are now included. On Twitter, about 20 parties whose collection stopped in late 2022 (the Irish parties, CDS-PP, PCP and CDU in Portugal, IU, Ciudadanos and Podemos in Spain, UDI and DLF in France, Plaid Cymru, the SNP, PvdA and VVD) now include 2023, most of them continuing to 2026, and JxCat, En Comú Podem, Katter's Australian Party and the five Canadian parties, listed as not collected, are included. Equo and the Social Democrats (Ireland) still end in 2022. Two filled periods have fewer posts than the months around them and may be incomplete: Hungary in January–June 2016 and Norway in 2015.
  • Twitter identifiers. Some tweet identifiers in version 1.0 had been corrupted (rounded, or written in scientific notation). About 15,700 tweets that version 1.0 counted twice, under their real id and under a rounded copy, are now counted once; this lowers post counts for Danish, French (2022), Portuguese and Norwegian parties. About 31,700 other tweets, mostly from Italian parties and the Lega in particular, appeared in version 1.0 only under a corrupted id; version 2.0 has them under their real id, and counts are unchanged.
  • Backdated Facebook posts. Version 1.0 includes 104 Facebook posts dated between 2000 and 2007, before Facebook pages existed (pages can publish posts with an earlier date). Because each party's series starts at its first post, the Facebook series of these parties (e.g. UMP, PS, ODS, SPÖ) start years too early in version 1.0, followed by long runs of empty months. Version 2.0 drops posts dated before the page was created. Its earliest Facebook post is from December 2007 (the Liberal Democrats, one month after Facebook introduced pages), and the other parties' series start in 2008 or later.
  • Scope. Four more countries, three more platforms, 49 more parties, and data to October 2026.

Classification

  • Policy filter. The policy classifier exists in two versions, both fine-tuned from xlm-roberta-base and differing in their training data. The first, mljn/policy-vs-other, was trained on about 15,700 annotated posts, most of them from nine countries (over 6,000 from France alone). The second, policy-vs-other-v2, was trained on 5,895 posts spread evenly over the 18 countries of version 1.0, about 330 per country; its F1 on held-out posts is 0.91, against 0.90 for the first. Version 1.0 used the first version for 15 countries and policy-vs-other-v2 for Czechia, Hungary and Poland, which were classified later. Version 2.0 uses policy-vs-other-v2 for all countries. In those 15 countries, 6–12% of posts move between policy and No Policy Content, and the share of No Policy Content is 2–8 points higher. Topic shares among policy posts change little.
  • Spanish posts. Version 2.0 removes full stops and commas from the text of Spanish parties' posts before the CAP classifier is applied. This step was introduced after classification errors were found in the Spanish data of version 1.0. There, Foreign Trade accounted for 3.8% of Spanish policy posts, against a median of 0.5% in the other countries (the highest being France, at 1.1%), and for some parties far more: 5.5% of the PNV's policy tweets, 4.4% of UPyD's. The cause is the classifier's training data: the Spanish texts the CAP classifier was trained on contain no full stops or commas, and Spanish posts classified with their punctuation are systematically pushed towards some topics, most visibly Foreign Trade and Government Operations. On the Spanish Twitter and Instagram posts of the hand-coded validation sample that are policy content (85 posts), the CAP classifier assigns the coders' topic to 74% of posts when they keep their punctuation and to 89% when full stops and commas are removed; in the other countries, removing them makes no difference (88% in both cases, 1,804 posts). In version 2.0 Foreign Trade accounts for 0.5% of Spanish policy posts, in line with the other countries. The step applies to every post of a Spanish party, including posts in Catalan, Basque and Galician, and only to the CAP classifier: the policy/no-policy decision is made on the original text. It is not applied in other countries, where it would make the labels worse. Compared with classifying the original text, it changes the topic of 12.3% of Spanish policy posts; Foreign Trade falls by about four fifths, while Civil Rights, Energy and Housing rise.
  • Facebook text. Facebook posts are classified on the message only, where version 1.0 also used image text and link titles and descriptions. The share of No Policy Content on Facebook rises from 34% to 46%.
  • Measures. Only the recoded measure is provided.

Effect on the measures

Figure 2 compares the two versions for every party, platform and year up to 2023 with at least 50 posts in both. Topic shares among policy posts agree closely: the correlation is 0.99 on Twitter and 0.95 on Facebook and Instagram, and the points lie around the diagonal. The share of No Policy Content is higher in version 2.0, slightly on Twitter (about 2 points on average) and Instagram (about 4 points), and clearly on Facebook (about 13 points), where the text classified changed. Almost all of these differences come from classification: computed on the same posts, the shares of version 2.0 differ from those of version 1.0 by the same amount, while the posts added in version 2.0 leave the shares of a party-year practically unchanged (correlation above 0.999). The topics that agree least are Foreign Trade (0.35, because of the Spanish correction), Culture (0.82) and Government Operations (0.88); by country, Spain (0.85) differs most, all other countries correlating at 0.96 or above.

Replication of the published results

To check whether the conclusions of the West European Politics paper depend on these changes, its analyses were run again with the topic shares of version 2.0, keeping everything else in the paper's data unchanged (party families, seat shares, polling, election dates) and using the same platforms and period as the paper. The paper's own code, run on its original data, reproduces the published estimates exactly.

  • Issue profiles of party families. The five most salient issues of each party family are the same in both versions for five of the six families, sometimes in a slightly different order. For social democratic parties, International Affairs falls from second to sixth place (4.5% to 4.0% of posts) and Social Welfare, sixth in version 1.0, enters the top five; the shares of all six issues lie within one point of each other in both versions.
  • Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the COVID-19 pandemic. The changes in attention around both events are almost identical: after February 2022, attention to Energy rises by 2.3 points in version 1.0 and 2.2 points in version 2.0, and Defense by 0.9 points in both; after the outbreak of the pandemic, attention to Health rises by 6.5 and 6.3 points.
  • Comparison with PARTYPRESS. Correlations between the two datasets' issue shares are the same in both versions (0.31 at the party–month–issue level and 0.44 at the party–year level for both, computed identically for the two versions).
  • Mainstream attention to immigration and radical right support. All three coefficients keep their sign and remain statistically significant (Figure 3). The effect of system-wide mainstream attention is 17.1 in version 2.0 against 15.3 in the paper, that of mainstream right attention 17.9 against 14.9, and that of mainstream left attention 12.4 against 13.7.
